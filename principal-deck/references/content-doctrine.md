# Content doctrine

The rules that make a deck defensible. These are the tests the content must
pass **before** any styling happens.

## Answer first, always

Every slide, every Q&A answer, every section opens with the conclusion. The
failure mode under pressure is *lede burial*: leading with the plumbing
(architecture, sequence, mechanism) instead of the business outcome. The fix
is structural: write the first sentence of each slide's talk track as the
thing the audience would ask for if they said "just give me the answer."

## The value grammar: baseline → projected → tracked

The single most-tested claim in any executive room is value. The grammar:

- **Baseline**: what the number is today. Where a process exists but is
  unmeasured, *measuring it is deliverable #1* and that measurement becomes
  the baseline. Where the capability is new, the old equivalent is the
  baseline (the batch journey baselines the real-time one; the unstitched
  metric baselines the stitched one).
- **Projected**: a defensible direction and rough magnitude — never a
  precise promise you cannot own. Refuse to promise a revenue percentage on
  a horizon you don't control; commit to leading indicators on short
  horizons and revenue direction on long ones.
- **Tracked**: the named KPI, its owner, and the forum where it is read
  (e.g. a monthly review with named attendees). A KPI without a forum is
  decoration.

**Attribution** must be designed, not asserted: holdout groups *prove*;
untouched markets/regions *indicate* (judged against the historic gap);
separate organic growth from capability-driven growth; pre/post comparisons
only where the process is fully yours.

**Lifecycle**: measure → iterate → retire. If a KPI disappoints, iterate once
(check the hypothesis and the content); still failing, the use case becomes
questionable and its capacity moves to the next-ranked item. Say out loud
which meeting takes that decision.

## Cost of inaction

Prepare the inversion explicitly: "waiting a year means…". Run-rate waste
continues, fragmentation deepens (N units doing things N different ways does
not get cheaper to fix later), and compounding starts later — delayed
compounding is never recovered. This is the answer to "why fund this now if
you won't promise year-one revenue."

## Positions, not vibes

The positions slide names the 2–4 decisions that determine success, each
with: the position, the rejected alternative, and the honest trade-off. A
position without a named rejection is a platitude. Classic shapes:

- **Thin slices over big-bang foundations**: every foundation increment is
  paid for by a use case that needs it. The scar story pattern: foundations
  built first, value promised later — later keeps moving.
- **Hub-plus-edges over pure central or pure local**: the hub packages and
  listens; the edges execute and feed back. "Central cannot know the
  problems the edges are facing."
- **A moving boundary with a fixed rule** (for AI/automation): capability is
  a moving frontier, so a static never-list goes stale; the rule that never
  breaks is that humans stay accountable. Concretize with today's list
  (irreversible actions stay human; no system widens its own permissions;
  secrets never go to the automation) and the escalation mechanics.

## Phases vs. use cases (the untangler)

**Phases build capability; use cases cash it in.** State each phase as what
it *earns* (trust on easy ground → the hard thing → scale without
headcount), and place each use case on the earliest phase whose capability
it needs. If the two look tangled, that's the thin-slice doctrine working —
say so.

## Risks, managed

3–4 risks maximum, each paired on the slide with its mitigation
(risk → mechanism). Promote them to visible cards; a risks footnote reads as
an afterthought and invites the "what keeps you up at night" ambush. The
spoken version is 20 seconds, at the end of the roadmap.

## Adoption as a first-class metric

Adoption is the leading indicator of value ("if adoption stalls, value
stalls two quarters later"). A usable model: a per-unit ladder
(readiness → activation → migration), time-per-rung as the metric, a
staleness rule (live but unused for 30 days = stale), and blockers played
back to the program lead so each unit's climb accelerates the next.

## Honesty rails

- Never invent a statistic. External numbers come from named studies with
  the commissioning party stated aloud.
- Personal experience claims at defensible strength only: "seen first-hand"
  vs. "delivered hands-on" are different claims; use the one that's true.
- Never name a client in a failure story unless explicitly cleared.
- Keep a written **kill-list** of banned phrases (debunked stats, guarantees,
  positions the presenter retracted) and check the deck against it.
- Anything the presenter can't own three questions deep stays out.

## Editorial bar

- Cut self-referential copy: the slide never talks about itself; footlines
  that explain the slide's own rhetoric get spoken, not written.
- Cut legends and labels the visual already explains.
- Every remaining component makes a strong case on its own.
- State assumptions as assumptions ("assuming a count in the twenties") —
  a correction from the room is a gift, take it.
