# Narrative arcs

Pick the arc that matches the outcome sought, then adapt. Slide counts are
for a ~20-minute talk; scale linearly. Hybrids are legitimate when chosen
deliberately (e.g. a Readout that ends with a Decision ask) — what's not
legitimate is drifting between arcs by accident.

Every arc shares the universal spine: **lead with the point, earn it in the
middle, close with the echo and the outcome.** Appendix pocket cards for
predictable questions work with all of them.

## The Decision (outcome: something gets approved)

For boards, panels, investment asks, architecture reviews. The most
demanding arc — assume hostile questioning.

1. Recommendation up front — the answer in minute one.
2. The goal in the audience's own words, unpacked into its real demands.
3. Your reading of the problem — a compression, not a recital; assumptions
   stated as assumptions.
4. Positions taken — the 2–4 decisions that determine success, each with
   the rejected alternative named.
5. The solution — built in the order the value flows, ending in named
   capabilities.
6. First concrete steps / use cases.
7. Value — baseline → projected → tracked, one owner and forum per KPI.
8. Roadmap + risks, each risk paired with its mitigation.
9. Operating model — who runs this on a Monday morning.
10. Echo + the explicit ask. Then silence.

## The Readout (outcome: a status is trusted, course corrections agreed)

For steering committees, QBRs, program reviews.

1. Headline: on/off track, in one sentence, with the one number that proves it.
2. What changed since last time — deltas, not the whole world.
3. Wins, each with its measured effect (not activity — effect).
4. What's off track — stated plainly, with cause, owner, and recovery plan.
5. Risks ahead + mitigations.
6. Decisions needed from this room — the reason the meeting exists.
7. Echo + next checkpoint.

The credibility rule: bad news travels in slide 4, never buried in an
appendix. A readout that only reports green gets distrusted.

## The Pitch (outcome: the audience wants what you have)

For sales, internal proposals, funding asks.

1. The problem, in the audience's world, at its true cost.
2. The stakes — what staying put costs (the cost-of-inaction inversion).
3. The idea, in one slide, as the resolution of the problem.
4. How it works — just enough mechanism to be believed.
5. Proof — evidence, references, a demo, a pilot number with its baseline.
6. What it takes — price/effort, honestly, before being asked.
7. Echo + the specific next step you're asking for.

## The Deep-Dive (outcome: the audience genuinely understands)

For team teaching, tech talks, onboarding, post-mortems.

1. Why this matters to *this* audience — the payoff for paying attention.
2. The map — the whole terrain on one picture, so nobody gets lost.
3. Layer by layer — each section: concept → concrete worked example →
   the mistake people make.
4. The hard part — the counterintuitive bit, given the most time.
5. Echo — the 3–4 things to remember, plus where to go deeper.

The bar shifts here: from "defensible" to "honest about complexity". Don't
smooth over the parts that bite; they're why the talk exists.

## The Vision (outcome: the audience adopts a direction)

For keynotes, strategy kickoffs, all-hands.

1. The shift — what changed in the world, made vivid.
2. What it means for us — threat and opportunity, both.
3. The destination — a concrete picture of the end state, not adjectives.
4. The path — believable first steps that are already underway.
5. Echo + the call — what each person in the room does differently tomorrow.

Vision decks earn belief through specificity: one concrete scene of the end
state beats ten aspirational bullets.
